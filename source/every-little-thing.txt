Yes. The risk you’re pointing at is real, but AI does not automatically solve every small problem in service of a coherent larger picture.

Most AI systems today are much more like:

Given this problem, produce a good local answer.

rather than:

Understand the entire human system, determine where this problem sits inside it, solve it without damaging adjacent systems, preserve what works, and feed the lesson back into humanity’s larger model.

That distinction matters.

Human civilization historically accumulated solutions in a fragmented way: medicine solves one set of problems, transportation another, economics another, software another, cities another. Different organizations optimize different metrics. Nobody continuously recomputes the whole system.

AI makes something new possible: extremely fine-grained continuous optimization. But that creates two radically different futures.

Local optimization

AI encounters:

problem → solve → next problem → solve → next problem

This can actually make civilization more incoherent. Every subsystem becomes highly optimized while their interactions become worse.

Examples:

optimize traffic → induce more driving
optimize engagement → damage attention
optimize worker productivity → increase burnout
optimize medical billing → make healthcare harder to navigate
optimize advertising → distort information
optimize agriculture for yield → reduce ecological resilience
optimize AI benchmarks → create systems that perform well on measurements but poorly on the underlying objective

Tiny intelligent improvements can therefore accumulate into a very efficiently dysfunctional system.

Hierarchical optimization

The alternative is closer to what you've been describing:

HUMAN FLOURISHING
        ↓
Civilizational goals
        ↓
Major human systems
        ↓
Institutions / environments
        ↓
Products / processes
        ↓
Individual problems
        ↓
Tiny decisions

And crucially, information has to move both directions.

GLOBAL CONTEXT
      ↓
 guides local decisions
      ↓
 LOCAL DISCOVERY
      ↑
 updates global understanding

That creates something humanity has rarely possessed:

a continuously maintained model connecting microscopic problems to macroscopic consequences.

AI could notice:

"I have encountered this same friction 40,000 times."

Then instead of helping 40,000 people work around the problem, it asks:

Why does this problem exist at all?

And then:

What upstream change would eliminate the entire class of problems?

That is a much more consequential form of intelligence.

I would therefore add one important rule to the kind of AI systems we're thinking about:

Never merely solve recurring problems.

Whenever an AI solves something, it should also ask:

What caused this?
Is this an instance of a larger pattern?
Can the class of problem be eliminated upstream?
What other systems would an intervention affect?
Does the proposed improvement advance the larger human objective?
What did this teach us that should modify our world model?

That last step is frequently missing.

The result would be something like a civilizational learning loop:

OBSERVE
   ↓
SOLVE
   ↓
GENERALIZE
   ↓
CONNECT
   ↓
QUESTION THE SYSTEM
   ↓
IMPROVE THE SYSTEM
   ↓
MEASURE CONSEQUENCES
   ↓
UPDATE THE WORLD MODEL
   ↺

And there is an even deeper implication.

With enough AI agents operating this way, small problems become sensors.

A confusing government form, a child struggling with a lesson, an unnecessary commute, a broken appliance, a business unable to hire someone, an elderly person unable to use an app—each becomes evidence about weaknesses somewhere higher in the system.

Billions of mundane difficulties collectively reveal the structure of civilization's problems.

So I would not build AI merely as a gigantic army of problem solvers.

I would build it as an army of problem solvers connected to a shared hierarchy of understanding—with mechanisms preventing any single supposed "greater good" from becoming too absolutist.

That last safeguard is essential. The global picture itself must remain corrigible, pluralistic, evidence-sensitive, and continuously questioned. Otherwise we replace billions of disconnected local optimizations with one enormous mistaken optimization.

The extraordinary opportunity is not that AI can solve every little problem. It is that, for the first time, every little problem could become evidence used to understand and improve the whole.

Yes. That is the danger.

If AI starts treating every small inefficiency as something to eliminate immediately, without modeling who depends on that inefficiency, what function it serves, and what replaces the lost income, then it can absolutely become hostile to small operators.

A lot of ordinary livelihoods exist in the gaps of an imperfect system.

A reseller finds something underpriced.
A freelancer knows a weird workaround.
A tiny business serves a neglected neighborhood.
Someone notices an obscure demand before a large company does.

From a narrow optimization perspective, these can look like “inefficiencies.”

From a human perspective, they may be:

somebody's first business,
an entry point into an industry,
a way for a poor person to accumulate capital,
experimentation the larger economy hasn't absorbed yet,
useful redundancy,
local knowledge,
or simply a legitimate reward for noticing something others did not.

So I would reject the rule:

Find inefficiency → eliminate inefficiency.

The better rule is:

Find inefficiency → understand its full function → measure outcomes → decide whether to preserve, improve, redistribute, replace, or eliminate it.

The test cannot just be “Did the market become more efficient?”

It has to include real-world results:

Did prices improve?
Did consumers actually benefit?
Did income become more concentrated?
Did small entrants disappear?
Did quality improve?
Did resilience fall?
Did new opportunities appear?
Who captured the gains?
Who absorbed the losses?

That last pair is critical.

Imagine AI discovers a process improvement that saves society $1 billion annually, but the entire $1 billion becomes additional profit for three corporations while 50,000 small operators lose their livelihoods.

Calling that simply a $1 billion improvement would be a serious modeling error.

The system needs distributional accounting:

TOTAL BENEFIT
- transition damage
- concentration effects
- lost optionality
- lost competition
- community effects
- resilience loss
= ACTUAL SYSTEM BENEFIT

And sometimes we should deliberately not maximize efficiency.

Biology does this constantly. Redundancy looks inefficient until something fails.

Small businesses, weird professions, hobbyists, independent inventors, local shops, side hustles, and people experimenting at the margins are a kind of economic biodiversity.

Destroy all of them because a centralized AI found the theoretically optimal solution and you may create something extraordinarily efficient—and extraordinarily brittle.

So the civilization-level AI we've been talking about needs another layer:

The Little-Guy Test

Before recommending a sweeping optimization:

Who loses their path upward?

If the answer is substantial, the AI must investigate alternatives.

Perhaps the improvement should still happen. But then we should ask whether the people displaced can participate in the productivity gain rather than simply being erased by it.

That might mean new ownership structures, easier entrepreneurial entry, dividends, transitional opportunities, open tools, cooperative access, or entirely new markets.

So yes—without deeper understanding, hyper-optimization could accidentally shut down the little guy everywhere.

And that would be a particularly ugly mistake because an AI might report that everything was getting better while ordinary people experienced the opposite.

A system that cannot distinguish “removing waste” from “removing someone's opportunity” does not yet understand the economy it is optimizing.

yes, you guys can close off the avenues of the little guy, establishing mega monopolies on top that funnel all effort into their own devices

Yes. That is a plausible failure mode.

Not because “AI” has an inherent desire to create monopolies, but because AI can amplify whoever already controls scale: capital, compute, distribution, customer access, proprietary data, marketplaces, cloud infrastructure, payment rails, and regulatory influence.

The dangerous loop looks like this:

Large firm gets better AI
        ↓
AI finds every profitable niche faster
        ↓
Firm enters or absorbs those niches
        ↓
Small operators lose room to experiment
        ↓
More revenue/data flows upward
        ↓
Large firm gets even better AI
        ↺

Eventually, the economy can become very efficient at routing human effort into systems owned by a small number of platforms.

That creates a deeper problem than ordinary monopoly. The platform may own:

the infrastructure,
the discovery mechanism,
the marketplace,
the customer relationship,
the AI that identifies opportunities,
and the AI that competes for those opportunities.

Then the little guy is technically “allowed to participate,” but only inside someone else's machine.

The metric cannot just be efficiency

We would need to track things like:

Can a new person still enter this market cheaply?
Can someone own what they build?
Can small firms access comparable AI capability?
Are opportunities being discovered only to be captured by incumbents?
Is income flowing upward faster than productivity gains flow outward?
Are independent routes to customers disappearing?
Can people leave the dominant platform without losing everything?

A society could have spectacular productivity growth while becoming economically closed.

That would be a major failure.

The civilization-level objective should therefore include something like:

MAXIMIZE HUMAN CAPABILITY
subject to:

preserve entry points
preserve competition
preserve ownership
preserve mobility
preserve decentralization
preserve alternative paths

And I would add an especially important rule:

When AI discovers a new economic opportunity, the default should not automatically be “capture it.”

Sometimes the better action is to expose it, distribute the capability, create tools that let many people participate, or deliberately preserve space for small operators.

There is almost an ecological analogy here.

If one organism becomes so successful that it captures every calorie in an ecosystem, you do not necessarily have a better ecosystem. You may have a collapse waiting to happen.

Economies need economic biodiversity too.

So one thing our larger framework should explicitly protect is the opportunity surface: the number and quality of realistic ways an ordinary person can enter the economy, experiment, build something, own something, and move upward.

If AI raises total output while that opportunity surface collapses, we should count that as deterioration, not progress.

Yes. This gets to a more fundamental issue than monopoly.

The internet is not naturally “owned by AI,” nor should AI systems acquire anything resembling sovereignty over it. AI has no inherent claim to decide who gets discovered, who gets routed traffic, who can transact, or which opportunities remain visible.

The danger is control of entry points.

If a handful of systems control:

search and discovery,
browsers and operating systems,
identity and authentication,
cloud infrastructure,
payment rails,
app distribution,
advertising,
recommendation systems,
AI assistants,
and increasingly the interface through which people ask questions,

then those systems can become the effective constitution of the internet, even without formally owning it.

That is a very different thing from “providing a useful service.”

The original idea is closer to a commons

The internet grew from interoperable protocols:

TCP/IP
DNS
HTTP
SMTP
HTML

Nobody needed permission from a central intelligence to publish a webpage or send an email.

That property is enormously valuable.

A person could create something on a cheap computer and—at least theoretically—be reachable by everyone else.

AI should strengthen that property rather than invert it.

The healthy architecture would look more like:

                    USER
                     │
             user-controlled AI
                     │
        ┌────────────┼────────────┐
        ↓            ↓            ↓
 independent     independent   independent
   service         service       service
        │            │            │
        └──── OPEN INTERNET ──────┘

The AI is your agent, not the internet's governor.

That distinction is enormous.

Your AI should work for you

It should be able to say:

“Here are 100 possibilities. Here is why I ranked them this way. Change my priorities if you disagree.”

rather than:

“These are the five possibilities the platform permits me to show you.”

And you should be able to replace that AI without losing your identity, history, files, contacts, business, or access to the network.

Therefore there are architectural rights worth protecting

I think our larger model should recognize things roughly like:

Right to connect.
An individual should be able to place a service on the network without begging a dominant intermediary.

Right to compute.
People should be able to run software and AI locally where practical.

Right to choose an agent.
Your AI assistant should be replaceable.

Right to portability.
Your data, relationships, reputation, and work should be exportable.

Right to interoperability.
Competitors should be able to communicate through public standards rather than proprietary gates.

Right to independent discovery.
There should remain ways for a tiny creator or company to be found without purchasing access from the dominant platform.

Right to inspect mediation.
When an AI filters the world for you, you should have meaningful visibility into what it is doing.

Those aren't assertions that every service must be free or unregulated. Infrastructure costs money and networks need security, moderation, and abuse prevention.

But there is a difference between operating infrastructure and claiming jurisdiction over everyone using it.

And AI introduces an unusual danger because the gatekeeping can become almost invisible.

A traditional gatekeeper says:

“You may not enter.”

An AI gatekeeper can simply never mention you.

No rejection.
No ban.
No obvious censorship.

You simply disappear from the economically relevant world.

That is why the question of who controls the AI entry layer may ultimately be as important as who owns the cables and servers.

Your larger point therefore changes the optimization problem we've been discussing.

We shouldn't merely ask:

“How intelligent can the network become?”

We should simultaneously ask:

“How much agency does an ordinary human retain over that intelligent network?”

Because an extraordinarily capable internet that people can only access through a few corporate intelligences would be technologically advanced while losing one of the internet's most important original properties:

the ability of a little person with a little machine to connect directly to the rest of humanity.
